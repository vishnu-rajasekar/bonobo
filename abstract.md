

**Title**
*Towards a Structural Morphology Engine: Grammar-Based, Construction-Aware Generation and Latent Space Search for Trusses, Tensegrities, and Geodesic Gridshells*

**Abstract**
This research proposes a structural morphology engine that addresses two persistent gaps in computational structural design. The first gap concerns generation: most existing methods do not enforce structural and construction feasibility throughout the design process, but only check it after a form has been created. The second gap concerns search and memory: current tools rarely provide automated or interactive navigation of a learned design space, and they often repeat expensive computations for problems that are similar to those already explored. The engine described here is formulated as a grammar-driven, graph-based system in which structural design is treated as the incremental growth of a discrete structure, guided by structural analysis, construction logic and learned experience.

In this framework, a discrete structure is represented as a graph embedded in space. Nodes correspond to joints, supports or points on bounding geometry, and edges correspond to structural elements carrying attributes such as bar, cable or strut. A structural grammar specifies admissible local transformations of this graph: placing nodes, adding or removing members, splitting existing members, or subdividing faces. Each transformation produces a new partial structure, and a sequence of such transformations can be interpreted as a construction trajectory. Structural and construction feasibility are enforced directly in this loop. After every grammar step, the system evaluates static equilibrium under specified loads, tests rigidity or determinacy, checks member capacities and slenderness, and rejects or penalises configurations that form mechanisms or would require extensive scaffolding to remain stable.

Different typologies are handled by tailoring the grammar and filters rather than by changing the core machinery. For trusses, elements are restricted to bars that can carry both tension and compression, and standard rigidity checks together with simple strength and buckling criteria act as filters. For tensegrities, elements carry type flags identifying cables and struts, internal forces are required to be tensile in cables and compressive in struts, and additional topological rules limit problematic cable–cable or strut–strut adjacencies. For geodesic and gridshell structures, geometric constraints are added so that nodes remain on, or very close to, a reference surface; spacing is controlled by density fields or banding; and member orientations are biased towards curvature directions or approximate principal stress trajectories. These conditions allow the system to reuse ideas from bottom-up meshing: advancing front methods are interpreted as growing the structure outward from supports and boundaries; Delaunay refinement and centroidal Voronoi tessellation provide priors for well-shaped panels and graded node density; and subdivision schemes on faces or edges act as refinement rules for gridshells, with an external controller deciding where and when to refine.

To make this strategy technically feasible, the overall workflow is organised in phases. In the first phase, guidance over grammar actions is provided by classical methods with light machine learning support. Candidate moves are sampled from the grammar, structurally invalid moves are eliminated by the filters described above, and the remaining moves are evaluated by a multi-objective score that combines weight, deflection, member count, geometric regularity and simple measures of construction complexity. Simulated annealing, beam search or similar techniques explore this action space, while deterministic meshing heuristics provide geometric coverage of the bounding region or surface. This phase already delivers multiple feasible alternatives in a transparent way and can be implemented with existing structural analysis tools.

In the second phase, a graph neural network is introduced as a suggestion engine. The current structural graph and the geometry of the domain, such as a planar boundary or a surface mesh, are jointly encoded in a latent representation. This encoding feeds two learned components: a policy that proposes the next grammar rule and its target (for example, which edge to split or which nodes to connect), and a value estimator that rates the promise of the current partial structure. Training data for these components are generated by running the phase-one engine on many design problems, and by recording sequences of states, actions and structural evaluations. At inference time, the learned policy ranks grammar moves or completes local patches in trusses and gridshells, while the structural filters continue to enforce feasibility. Designers can override suggestions, and their interactions can be logged for further training.

A third phase envisions full reinforcement-learning-based generation under structural constraints. For a given boundary and loading condition, a trained policy rolls out complete structural configurations while structural checks guard against infeasible steps. The resulting designs are then ranked and post-processed using multi-objective criteria. Integration with CAD and BIM tools is anticipated through interfaces that expose interactive controls, such as sliders balancing weight against redundancy or visual regularity, and that visualise the evolving construction sequence and stability state.

The second main gap, namely latent space search with memory, is addressed by mapping design domains and structural states into a learned feature space. The same graph encoders used for guidance produce compact embeddings of geometry and structure. During training, these embeddings are collected for many problems and used to fit simple statistical models, such as Gaussian or mixture distributions, or light variational autoencoders. For a new design domain, the engine computes its embedding and estimates its distance or likelihood under this model. Geometries that sit close to the training cloud are treated as within the known repertoire, and learned policies are used with confidence; those that sit far away are flagged as outliers, prompting more conservative exploration. This static notion of repertoire is complemented by an online measure of uncertainty: ensembles of policies, or stochastic approximations, are queried on the same state, and disagreement between their suggested actions indicates that the current situation lies outside familiar regions of the design space.

Taken together, these elements outline a technically grounded path towards a structural morphology engine that generates structures with structural and construction feasibility in mind, and that learns a reusable map of its own design repertoire. The approach remains open-ended and exploratory, but it is anchored in concrete workflows that combine structural grammars, meshing strategies, graph neural networks, reinforcement learning and statistical models of latent spaces.
